Archibald, the system that runs my admin

In April 2026 I started building an AI agent system to run my own finances, email and schedule. I had no engineering background.

Personal project · Builder · April 2026 to present

186

jobs created since June, one at a time; more than 140 are live today

I built it for two reasons. My own admin, from bills and statements to email and scheduling, was taking hours every week. And I wanted to understand AI agents by building one, not by reading about them. My own life was the test case.

Archibald runs only my own accounts. Otian AI grew out of it: Archie, the product we sell, takes what I learned building Archibald and puts it to work for small businesses.

What it does

It works on a schedule, mostly without me. It pulls transactions from six bank and card feeds, reconciles them, checks statements and closes each month. It also sorts my email and keeps my calendar. That gives me back about five hours a week.

It runs on OpenClaw, on a Mac mini. I built it in Claude Code and Codex, in Python. Its skills have automated tests that catch regressions, and guardrails limit what it can do without asking me first.

Plain code first

Most of Archibald isn’t AI at all. 144 of its 145 live jobs are plain scripts that run without a model. The AI handles conversation and judgment calls, and anything that can be a rule is a rule. That keeps it cheap and predictable: a script does the same thing every time.

Cost gets the same attention. When daily spend crept from $1.50 to $6.30, I traced it to a routine check that reloaded a growing conversation every 30 minutes. Trimming what it reloaded cut that check’s usage by about 85%, and a daily monitor now alerts me if spend climbs again.

Guardrails in code, not instructions

Telling an AI model a rule works most of the time. Most of the time isn’t enough for email, money or my accounts. So I follow one rule: if Archibald breaks an instruction twice, the instruction is replaced by code that checks the action before it runs. There are now ten of these guards. A few of them:

  • Email. Nothing goes out in my name until I approve that exact draft. The approval works once, for 15 minutes, and breaks if the draft changes.
  • Trades. Every brokerage order waits for me to tap “Allow once.” No answer means no order, and no scheduled job can place one.
  • My data. Anything that could send data off the Mac is checked first. Before that guard went live, I replayed 33,759 past commands through it to confirm it blocked only what it should.
  • Its own rules. Archibald can’t edit its own rules or settings, or switch off a guard.
  • Honest replies. If it tells me something is saved or booked, it has to have actually run that step. An audit of 366 of its replies found that every wrong number came from the model answering from memory.

Button taps work the same way. When I tap Approve on my phone, code handles it directly instead of asking the model to interpret it.

When “ok” wasn’t true

In October a morning job stopped running for two days. The nightly job that keeps it on the schedule reported “ok” three nights in a row.

The check confirmed that the scheduling command had worked. It never confirmed that the job would run. The job had been switched off by hand, and the command will update a switched-off job without complaint.

I changed the check to confirm the job will actually run, and added an alert after the first failure. Several of its jobs now exist only to check its own work, including a weekly accuracy check and daily finance controls.

It wasn’t the only time. A few more from this fall:

  • The lights’ cloud service reported “ok” for commands that never reached the lights. I switched to controlling them directly over the home network.
  • A backup had been failing quietly because the sync folder wouldn’t overwrite the old file. Now every backup is a new file that gets unlocked and checked, and old copies are deleted only after the new one passes.
  • Some test files were never actually running, which hid hundreds of checks, and one had been failing for days. I found three separate ways a test suite can silently stop running and closed each one. A full self-check now runs every morning.

It’s the same lesson as the $1.8M power bill. A report that says everything matches isn’t proof. Check the thing itself.
