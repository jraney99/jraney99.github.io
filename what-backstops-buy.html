What a Google backstop buys

Two bitcoin miners raised nearly $5 billion of secured debt against leases to a young AI cloud. The buildings didn’t make that possible. The guarantor did.

October 2026

At Thorofare Capital I underwrote commercial real estate loans. The question I kept coming back to was simple: who pays the rent, and what happens if they stop? AI data centers have made that question worth billions of dollars.

Two deals, one structure

In August 2025, TeraWulf signed two 10-year leases with Fluidstack, an AI cloud provider, for more than 200 MW of critical IT load at its Lake Mariner campus in New York, worth about $3.7 billion over the initial terms. Google agreed to backstop $1.8 billion of Fluidstack’s lease obligations. Days later a 160 MW expansion lifted contracted revenue to about $6.7 billion and added $1.4 billion to Google’s backstop, bringing it to about $3.2 billion.

In September 2025, Cipher Mining signed a similar 10-year lease with Fluidstack for 168 MW at Barber Lake in Texas, worth about $3 billion. Google backstopped $1.4 billion of it.

What the guarantee does

TeraWulf’s filing spells out the mechanics. If Fluidstack defaults or becomes insolvent, Google either pays a termination fee or pays the rent due and takes over the lease. The backstop starts when each lease starts. Until then, Google pledges its warrants to the lenders financing construction.

So a lender is not really underwriting a young AI cloud. It is underwriting a floor under that tenant, set by one of the strongest balance sheets in the world.

What it unlocked

Within about two months, both companies sold senior secured notes against the projects: $3.2 billion at 7.750% for TeraWulf’s subsidiary in October 2025, and $1.4 billion at 7.125% for Cipher’s in November, followed by a $333 million add-on. Both note deals are secured in part by a lockbox account for Fluidstack’s payments and, until construction is finished, by Google’s pledged warrants.

What it cost

Google was paid in equity: warrants for about 14% of TeraWulf across both deals and about 5.4% of Cipher. That equity is the price of borrowing someone else’s credit.

Credit is the product

The land, the power and the cooling are what it takes to be in the game. What a lender pays for is the rent check, and the rent check is only as good as whoever stands behind it. In both deals the backstop covered about half of the initial contracted revenue. That is a floor under the rent, not the whole rent, and the difference is where the real underwriting happens. Now look at the debt next to the guarantee. TeraWulf’s $3.2 billion of notes matches Google’s $3.2 billion total backstop, and Cipher’s $1.73 billion is about 81% backstopped. The lenders sized the debt to the guarantee, not to the buildings.

What I’d check on any data center lease

  • Who ultimately pays the rent, and what is their credit?
  • If there is a backstop, how much of the rent does it cover, when does it start, and what triggers it?
  • What happens if the building is late, before the guarantee is live?
  • Does the lease outlast the debt? Here, 10-year leases sit under notes due in 2030.
  • Who pays for power, taxes and repairs: the tenant, or the landlord out of rent?
  • What does the guarantor get in return, and who carries the dilution?

Sources: TeraWulf’s August 14, 2025 announcement, expansion announcement and notes pricing; Cipher Mining’s September 25, 2025 announcement, notes pricing and add-on pricing. Implied rent and backstop shares are my arithmetic on those figures.
