Downside first

The most important question I learned underwriting loans, and how it followed me from real estate debt to bitcoin mining to a startup.

October 2026

I started my career underwriting commercial real estate loans at Thorofare Capital, more than 50 bridge, mezzanine and construction deals. The most important question on every one was the same. Before asking how a deal makes money, ask how it loses money, and how much.

Looking at the downside first was the part of lending I found most interesting. It has stayed with me in every job since.

At MARA

At MARA the same question kept coming up in different forms. On our power bills, it meant assuming an invoice could be wrong and checking it against our own data, which is how we found $1.8 million in billing errors. On hedging and curtailment, it meant asking what happens to a site when power prices spike. And in modeling what it would take to convert mining sites to AI and HPC, it meant asking what happens if things don’t go the way the base case says.

Build the model yourself

The other thing underwriting taught me was to build the model myself. When you build it, you know every assumption in it and which ones the answer depends on. I did the same at MARA, with one cost model for all 18 sites, and at Otian, where our 12-month growth model has a floor case alongside the plan.

Now

With my own money, I’m more fearless than you might expect from a former underwriter. With bitcoin, I saw where it was headed and what inflation was doing to the U.S. dollar, and I invested with conviction. Downside first doesn’t mean avoiding risk. It means knowing what you could lose before you decide a bet is worth it.

At Otian, the downside question is still there. We bootstrapped it with no outside money, and we run it so that a bad month can’t sink us.

The deals, the assets and the industries have changed. The first question hasn’t.
