Where I stand

4.1Handicap Index

My index is 4.1, I shoot about 82 on the rounds I post, and on the 5 rounds where every shot was recorded I averaged 85.8, which is 10 strokes a round worse than a scratch player would be from the same places.

Those three numbers disagree on purpose, and that is the first thing worth understanding about my own data.

Handicap Index
What a Handicap Index is. Not an average. Each round becomes a differential, which is the score adjusted for how hard that course plays. The index is the average of the best 8 of the last 20 differentials, so it describes my better days, not my typical ones. That is why my index is lower than my scoring average, and why both are true at once.

Hole by hole, those rounds were 4 birdies and 1 eagle, 29 pars, 30 bogeys and 17 doubles or worse across 81 holes. The doubles are 21% of my holes and they are where the round actually goes. Nothing on this page matters as much as how those holes start and end.

How one shot becomes a handicap

Everything on this site comes out of one chain. Each link is simple; the confusion comes from meeting them out of order.

  1. 1
    Every shotstart and end position, recorded by the sensors in my grips
  2. 2
    Expected strokeswhat a scratch player would need from each of those positions
  3. 3
    Strokes gained, per shotthe difference the shot made, against that standard
  4. 4
    Four categoriesdriving, approach, short game, putting: every shot lands in one
  5. 5
    The gap10 strokes a round, which is the four added up
  6. 6
    Leaksthe distance buckets inside those categories that are actually losing strokes
  7. 7
    Practicethe sessions are built from the leak table, in order
Expected strokes
Expected strokes, the idea everything else is built on. From any spot on a course there is an average number of strokes it takes to hole out. From 150 yards in the fairway a scratch player averages about 2.9. From 15 feet, about 1.8. Those averages are the measuring stick: every shot moves me from one expected number to another.
Strokes gained
Strokes gained, in one sentence. Take the expected strokes where the ball started, subtract the expected strokes where it ended, subtract 1 for the shot I just played. Positive means I gained ground on scratch; negative means I lost it. A 150 yard approach to 15 feet is 2.9 − 1.8 − 1 = +0.1: slightly better than par for that shot.
Why they add up
Why the four categories add up to the total. Every shot belongs to exactly one category, decided by where it was played from: off the tee on a par 4 or 5 is driving, a full shot to the green is approach, anything inside 50 yards that is not a putt is short game, and a putt is putting. Add the four up and you get the whole gap to scratch, which is why they always sum to the total.

The handicap is a separate chain that runs alongside it, built from scores rather than shots. That is why rounds I played without the sensors still move my index but never appear on the strokes gained pages.

Differential
Differential. One round’s score, adjusted for course difficulty, so a 79 on a hard course can beat a 76 on an easy one. A differential at or under my index means that round beat my handicap.
Scratch
Who "scratch" is here. A 0.0 handicap player. Every comparison on these pages is against what that player would be expected to do from the same place on the course, not against tour professionals.

The four parts, and how they feed each other

Four parts, in the order the shots happen. Two of them are established findings and two are not yet, which matters more than the headline numbers.

The ranges
Why every number has a range. These come from a handful of rounds, so each figure is an estimate. The range is the band the true number is 95% likely to sit in. When the range crosses zero, I genuinely cannot tell that part of my game apart from scratch yet, no matter what the headline number says. Those get called "not callable", and they never set the practice agenda.

Driving

−3.2 to +0.3 a round not callable yet

I hit 26 of 63 fairways and my driver goes 303 yards, which is not the problem. The problem is 10 penalty balls off the tee in 63 tee shots. Distance I have; the ball being in play I do not.

Penalty cost
What a penalty shot costs. Measured, not assumed: tee shots that took a penalty are compared against my clean tee shots on the same holes. The difference is what one costs.

Approach

−3.1

−5.3 to −0.9 a round established

I hit 33% of greens against about 52% for scratch, and when I do hit one I am 26 feet away. No single yardage stands out as the villain. What does stand out is the direction: 25 of my 49 misses are short, more than every other miss put together, and the pattern sits left, short.

Greens in regulation
Green in regulation. On the green in two shots fewer than par: one shot on a par 3, two on a par 4, three on a par 5. Miss it and I need an up and down to save par.
Proximity
Proximity. How far from the hole an approach finishes, measured as the middle value rather than the average so one shank does not move it. Closer means shorter putts, which is most of what putting is.
Dispersion
Dispersion and miss tendency. Lateral is left and right spread, depth is long and short. Both are standard deviations in yards: roughly two thirds of shots land inside that figure. The tendency is where the middle of the pattern sits, which says more about club selection and aim than any single miss.

Short game

−4.2

−6.2 to −2.1 a round established

This is the biggest single hole in my game and it follows directly from the one above. Missing 67% of greens means about 12 chances a round to save par with a chip and a putt. I convert 29% of the ones inside 30 yards, 9 of 31, where scratch converts about 54%. From 30 to 50 yards I am 0 of 16.

Up and down
Up and down. Missed the green, then holed out in two more shots: one chip or pitch, one putt. The rate here counts only shots from inside 30 yards, which is what a scratch comparison is built on. The 30 to 50 yard pitches are reported separately, because mixing them in flatters the number.

Putting

−1.2

−3.3 to +0.8 a round not callable yet

I take 32 putts a round, which sounds bad until you remember I am putting from further away than a scratch player because of everything above. My 8 three-putts followed first putts averaging 39 feet, against 19 feet on the holes I two-putted.

Three-putts
Three-putts. Three or more putts on one hole. What matters is what preceded them: the length of the first putt. The data records where each putt started but not how far the second one was left, so I can say long first putts come before three-putts, and not whether the lag or the short one was the miss.

Leaks
What a "leak" is, and what the share means. A bucket of shots that is measurably costing strokes against scratch, priced per round. The share is how much of the total available improvement sits in that one bucket, so a 41% share means fixing it is worth 41% of everything on the table.
Too few to read
"Too few to read". A distance bucket with fewer than 8 shots behind it is greyed out and never chosen as a focus area. Three good chips in a row is not evidence. Under 3 shots it is not published at all.
How practice follows
How practice follows from this. The leak table is read straight into my practice sessions: the top leaks become the blocks I hit, and the session is built around them rather than around whatever I feel like practising. That is also why a bucket with too few shots is never allowed to rank, because a fluke would set the agenda for weeks.

What I am working on, and why

Three things, in this order, because this is the order the data puts them in. Each one has a number attached, so it is possible to be wrong about it later.

  1. 1
    Put the tee ball in play10 penalty tee shots in 63 swings is 2.2 a round. Halving that to 5 is worth about 1.1 strokes a round on its own.16% of tee shots → 8%
  2. 2
    Get up and down from inside 30 yards29% now against about 54% for scratch. Getting to 41% would have saved 4 shots over these rounds, about 0.8 a round.29% → 41%
  3. 3
    Take enough club25 of 49 approach misses are short. That is a club selection habit, not a swing fault, and it is the cheapest thing on this list to change. It also feeds the item above: short misses leave the longest chips.one more club

The two I can put a number on are worth about 1.9 strokes a round between them, which turns the 86 I average with shots tracked into an 84. Closing those buckets all the way to scratch would be 5.3, but that is the ceiling rather than a plan.

Nothing here says swing differently. Everything here says play differently and practise the two shots that keep costing me, which is what the practice plans are built from.

What this data cannot tell me

This is built on 5 rounds and 81 holes. That is enough to be useful and not enough to be certain, and the honest version of this page says which is which.

Two of the four parts are not callable yet
Driving and putting both have ranges that still cross zero. They may be fine, they may be bad, and more rounds will say.
Every lie is assumed
The data records where the ball was, not what it was sitting on. A chip from thick rough and a chip from the fairway are scored the same, so my short game number is probably a little harsher than the truth.
Distances are raw GPS
Mine are measured from where the ball started to where it stopped, so they include roll. The figures inside the Arccos app are modelled and run longer.
Some rounds are missing
Only the rounds where I wore the sensors are here. Rounds without them still move my handicap and still appear on the rounds page, but they cannot be broken down.
The scratch baseline is calibrated, not published
The expected-strokes table behind all of this was assembled from public sources and tuned to agree with Arccos on these same rounds, so it is a reasonable standard rather than an independent one.

When any of that changes, these pages change with it. They rebuild from the data every morning, so nothing here is a claim I wrote once and left standing.
